Voice & Tone

How Kalix writes

Kalix is a serious tool for people who know their field. The writing should read that way: plain, honest, and quiet. We describe what the software does and trust the reader to decide whether it is useful to them.

01

Six principles

1

Plain words win.

Prefer the short, common word to the long one. Omit needless words. If a sentence works with three words cut, cut them. utilise → use · in order to → to · functionality → features

2

State, don't sell.

No hard calls to action, no urgency, no arm-twisting. Say what the tool does and where to find more. The reader is a professional deciding on a tool — not a lead to convert.

3

Back it, or cut it.

A superlative you can prove is fair game — pair it with a benchmark, a number, or a citation (blazing-fast is fine sitting next to the figure that shows it). It's the unbacked claim that reads as noise. And drop the adjectives that can't be measured at all — powerful, seamless, revolutionary — there is nothing there to back.

4

Active and present.

Write in the active voice and the present tense. "models can be run by Kalix" → "Kalix runs models"

5

Respect the reader.

They are hydrologists and engineers. Be precise, use the correct term, and don't explain the obvious. Precision is a courtesy, not a flex.

6

Be honest about scope.

Kalix is early (v0.2.0). Say what it does today. Mark planned features plainly as planned; never imply something exists when it doesn't.

03

Empty adjectives

Unmeasurable by nature — there's nothing to back, so cut them. A claim you can prove (runs in seconds, or blazing-fast beside a benchmark) is always welcome.

powerful cutting-edge seamless revolutionary world-class effortless next-generation supercharge unlock game-changing

If a phrase would sound at home in a product-launch tweet, cut it. If you can put a number beside it, keep the number.
